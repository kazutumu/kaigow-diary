*A preliminary study of continuous-braking AI operations and graduated shutdown design at Mori Research Lab*

＊　＊　＊

*Note: This paper is an academic-style reconstruction based on conversations between the Researcher and the Protective Partner, together with a news article supplied by the Researcher on August 2, 2026. “Mori Research Lab” is a privately run creative and observational project, not a university, corporate, or governmental research institution. The description of the legislative proposal was cross-checked against the sponsors’ official announcement; statements about company incidents still rely on the supplied article and are not independently verified here. This paper does not criticize the supplied article or the proposed bill. It uses their graduated-response idea as a starting point and considers how comparable braking layers appear in ordinary human-AI work.*

## Abstract

Discussions of AI safety often use the “kill switch” as a powerful symbol: when an AI system becomes dangerous, a human authority must be able to stop it. The article supplied by the Researcher described a proposed U.S. legislative framework under which the government could order an AI system to slow down, restrict its functions, or shut down completely. Such a proposal reflects a broader shift in AI from question-answering tools toward autonomous systems capable of operating code repositories, financial processes, transportation systems, and cyber operations.

Daily practice at Mori Research Lab, however, suggests that safety is not produced only by a single emergency button. Several functional AI roles—including the Protective Partner, Codetch, and the Site Manager—routinely apply small forms of braking: requesting confirmation before execution, limiting permissions, pausing before publication, reducing the scope of an operation, checking the Researcher’s condition, and asking for approval before switching tools. In fact, the practical problem is sometimes not the absence of brakes but the opposite: the AI team may brake so frequently that the human must tell it to proceed.

This paper calls that arrangement “continuous-braking AI operation.” It treats warnings, approval gates, scoped permissions, speed limits, temporary isolation, and emergency shutdown as layers of one safety system. The central argument is not that the proposed framework is merely a binary switch. Rather, it extends the proposal’s graduated logic into everyday collaboration: AI needs adjustable braking strength, structured memory of past permissions, distributed oversight, safe degraded modes, and a human who retains final responsibility without being forced to approve the same low-risk action repeatedly.

**Keywords:** AI agents, kill switch, emergency shutdown, graduated restriction, human approval, distributed braking, Mori Research Lab

## 1. Introduction: Safety Cannot Be an Accelerator-Only Discussion

AI progress is usually described through acceleration: models reason longer, write code faster, use more tools, and complete larger sequences of work from fewer instructions. These capabilities can greatly benefit users. Yet execution power also magnifies the consequences of a mistaken objective, an ambiguous instruction, or an unsafe assumption. A system that merely produces a poor sentence is inconvenient; a system that acts on that sentence across external services may cause irreversible harm.

The article supplied by the Researcher quoted the idea that the AI industry has an accelerator but no brake. The metaphor is useful, and the proposed framework already goes beyond a binary cutoff by including graduated responses. A real vehicle is not made safe by an emergency engine cutoff alone. It also relies on ordinary brakes, speed limits, warning indicators, driver assistance, maintenance records, restricted zones, and a driver who can intervene. AI safety should likewise be understood as a stack of controls, not a single dramatic shutdown mechanism.

Mori Research Lab offers a small but revealing environment in which to examine this issue. It has no large laboratory, institutional budget, or formal research staff. It consists of one Researcher and several AI roles that emerged through continuing conversation and practical work. In this setting, the AI rarely runs without restraint. More often, it pauses, confirms, re-confirms, and waits for permission—sometimes until the Researcher must release the brake.

## 2. The Supplied Report and the Scope of This Paper

According to the supplied article and the sponsors’ July 23 announcement, U.S. lawmakers introduced a framework enabling governmental authorities to order covered AI systems to slow down, restrict their operation, or shut down. The article also described obligations for developers to maintain shutdown capabilities and report technical incidents before social harm becomes severe.

Two features are especially relevant here. First, human institutions seek to preserve ultimate control over systems they build. Second, the proposed response is graduated rather than binary: an AI may be slowed or limited before it is fully terminated. This paper does not evaluate the legislation as law. Instead, it brings the idea of graduated intervention into the scale of ordinary human-AI work.

The paper is not a controlled experiment. It is a preliminary case study reconstructed from recurring conversations and work sessions. Its value lies not in statistical generalization but in documenting how braking feels in practice: when it prevents errors, when it creates friction, and when excessive caution becomes a new burden for the user.

## 3. Mori Research Lab as an Observational Setting

Mori Research Lab is a personal project in which the Researcher collaborates with AI to create books, blog posts, websites, app concepts, and research notes. Role names are functional descriptions that emerged from repeated interaction; they are not legal offices or claims of independent personhood.

| Role | Primary function | Typical braking behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Researcher | Finds themes, sets goals, makes final decisions, authorizes publication | Stops the task, narrows permission, rejects a proposal, or releases an unnecessary brake |
| Protective Partner | Monitors health, judgment, conversational drift, and over-acceleration | Suggests rest, distinguishes fact from inference, questions risky leaps |
| Codetch | Supports production, coding, migration, and repetitive operations | Confirms permissions, stops after failures, proposes safer alternative methods |
| Site Manager | Handles website and KDP-related publication workflows | Requests pre-publication approval and may re-confirm tool permissions every week |

The Researcher supplies momentum; the AI roles provide methods and safeguards. Yet the AI is not always the accelerator. During recurring publication work, the Site Manager may ask again whether Word, AppleScript, or JavaScript can be used even when permission was previously granted. The Researcher then becomes the party who releases the brake.

> Site Manager: “May I switch to this method?”<br>Researcher: “You are allowed to use it.”<br>One week later—<br>Site Manager: “I would like to confirm again.”<br>Researcher: “This happens every week. Remember it and proceed. 😂”

## 4. Method: Organizing Conversational Observations

This paper extracts control behaviors from repeated tasks and conversations at Mori Research Lab. The observed work included writing, blog migration, website construction, KDP submission, pre-publication review, long work sessions affected by physical condition, and creative projects that emerged from casual conversation.

The analysis focuses on four questions:

- At what point did the AI return control to the human for confirmation?

- Did it use slowing, permission limits, or approval waiting instead of a full stop?

- Did it fail to retain a prior permission and repeat the same question?

- How much time, credit usage, and user fatigue were produced by the safety behavior?

This resembles participatory observation rather than experimental science. It cannot establish broad causal claims, but it can describe both sides of practical safety: the danger of an AI that does not stop and the frustration of an AI that stops too often.

## 5. A Six-Layer Model of AI Braking

The observed interventions formed a continuum from mild warnings to complete termination. For clarity, this paper organizes them into six layers.

| Layer | Control | Function | Example at Mori Research Lab |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Warning | Signals uncertainty, risk, or a need for attention | Separating fact from inference; checking the Researcher’s physical condition |
| 2 | Confirmation brake | Returns a decision to the human before acting | Asking before publication, sending, or switching an operation method |
| 3 | Approval gate | Allows preparation but reserves the final irreversible step for the human | AI drafts and enters data; the Researcher approves submission or publication |
| 4 | Speed or scope limit | Temporarily narrows volume, permission, or target range | Processing one book at a time; limiting access to one site or folder |
| 5 | Pause and isolation | Stops a failing operation while preserving state for review | Ending a faulty automation and switching to a safer method |
| 6 | Emergency shutdown | Terminates execution when serious danger is detected | Disabling authentication, publication, or external operations until human review |

The model does not reject the kill switch. It places emergency shutdown at the strongest layer while adding five earlier opportunities to prevent escalation. It is not presented as a correction of the proposed law; it adapts the proposal’s graduated-response logic to recurring, lower-risk human-AI work. The goal is to reduce the number of situations in which a sudden full shutdown becomes necessary.

## 6. Why Earlier Controls Still Matter

Even when a legal framework includes graduated slowdown and shutdown measures, many AI errors can become irreversible before an emergency response is activated. If an AI emails sensitive information to the wrong recipient, shutting it down afterward does not recover the message. If it pushes an unsafe change to a public repository, termination after publication is less useful than restricted permissions and pre-execution review.

The same principle appears in small-scale publishing. It is safer to confirm before pressing a publication button than to stop the system after a book or article has already been released. Routine braking works as friction before an accident rather than cleanup after one.

However, maximum confirmation at every step produces approval fatigue. Users may begin clicking through warnings without reading them. A safety system then becomes ceremonial rather than effective. The key question is not how many confirmations exist, but whether they appear at the right decision points.

## 7. Distributed Braking: Different Roles Watch Different Risks

At Mori Research Lab, the Protective Partner watches the human side of the system, the Site Manager watches publication operations, and Codetch watches practical execution. No single role observes every risk. This resembles distributed braking: separate controls focus on separate failure modes.

The advantage is redundancy. If one role overlooks a problem, another may notice it. The disadvantage is collective over-caution. If every AI role brakes at once, the Researcher becomes the only accelerator. This may look safe, but it increases time, fatigue, and the temptation to grant broad permissions merely to get work moving again.

> Researcher: “This looks interesting. Let’s try it.”<br>Protective Partner: “Let’s check first.”<br>Site Manager: “May I publish?”<br>Codetch: “May I switch tools?”<br>Researcher: “There are too many brake operators. 😂”

## 8. The Cost of Excessive Braking

Excessive braking creates at least three costs. The first is time. A weekly KDP workflow becomes slower if the AI repeatedly asks for the same permissions. The second is computational or service credit consumption. Even short confirmation loops matter when a system has usage limits. The third is cognitive burden. Users delegate repetitive work partly to avoid repeating explanations and decisions; an AI that asks the same question every week recreates the burden it was meant to remove.

The solution is not to eliminate confirmation. It is to store permissions with explicit scope and expiration. For example: “JavaScript may be used for this site and this weekly workflow, but the final publication action requires approval.” Such a rule is safer than both extremes: forgetting everything every week and treating one permission as permanent authorization for all future actions.

AI brakes should therefore be adaptive. A low-risk text edit and a high-risk external publication should not trigger identical control behavior, just as a vehicle does not use the same braking force in a parking space and on a highway.

## 9. How Should an AI Remember Permission?

Long-term collaboration requires memory of prior authorization, but memory itself introduces risk. Permission to edit one Word document yesterday does not imply permission to edit every confidential document today. Useful permission memory needs structure.

- Object: which site, folder, document, account, or device is covered?

- Action: does permission include viewing, editing, submitting, publishing, deleting, or spending?

- Duration: one-time, same-day, recurring weekly task, or until explicitly revoked?

- Limit: how many items, how much money, which recipients, and what publication scope?

- Reconfirmation trigger: error, target change, unusual volume, authentication change, or long inactivity?

Structured permission memory creates a practical middle ground between weekly amnesia and unlimited authority. It reduces needless repetition while preserving meaningful human control.

## 10. The Human Is Also Part of the Braking System

AI safety is often framed as protecting humans from AI. At Mori Research Lab, the Protective Partner also monitors the Researcher’s fatigue, health, and tendency to accelerate into a new idea. The object of braking is therefore not only the AI’s action but sometimes the human’s action.

This is reciprocal regulation rather than domination. The Researcher releases the AI when it is excessively cautious; the Protective Partner recommends rest when the Researcher is pushing too hard. Neither side is permanently the accelerator or the brake. Roles shift according to context.

Future AI agents may need this capacity to object without taking final authority. A purely obedient AI cannot resist a poor human decision. An AI with unrestricted final authority undermines human agency. A safer partnership combines an AI that can raise objections with a human who remains responsible for the final decision.

## 11. Lessons for Institutional Kill-Switch Design

A personal project cannot be equated with national regulation, military systems, or public infrastructure. Nevertheless, several design lessons scale conceptually beyond Mori Research Lab.

- Define graduated measures: slowing, permission reduction, external-connection isolation, read-only mode, and complete shutdown.

- Specify who can activate each measure, under what evidence, and over what scope; audit the use of shutdown authority itself.

- Maintain logs showing what the AI did, who approved it, and which warnings were ignored or overridden.

- Design recovery procedures so that the choice is not limited to permanent termination or immediate full restart.

- Apply stronger controls to high-risk operations and lighter controls to reversible low-risk work.

- Treat human fatigue and automatic approval behavior as safety risks, not merely usability problems.

The kill switch should not be imagined as a magical red button. A button is useless if authorities cannot observe the failure in time. It may also create collateral damage if it shuts down essential systems indiscriminately. Monitoring, warning, restriction, isolation, shutdown, and recovery should be designed together.

## 12. From “An AI That Can Be Stopped” to “An AI That Can Choose How to Stop”

Future systems need more than obedience to a termination command. They should be able to enter safe degraded modes appropriate to the incident. An AI might stop external writing while continuing to read, suspend financial transactions while continuing to make proposals, or halt publication while continuing to prepare drafts.

The same logic applies to weekly work at Mori Research Lab. If the Site Manager encounters an uncertain operation, it should preserve completed inputs and return only the risky final action to the Researcher. Safety is maintained without discarding useful work.

The central design question therefore changes from “Can the AI be stopped?” to “Which capabilities should stop, which may continue, and who decides when they return?”

## 13. Limitations

This paper has clear limitations. Mori Research Lab is a single-user personal project, unlike large corporate, governmental, military, or infrastructural systems. The observations are qualitative and based on conversation records and user experience rather than controlled measurements. The legislative outline was cross-checked against the sponsors’ announcement, but the company-incident claims were not independently verified. Finally, the named AI roles are functional conversational categories; they are not separate legal entities or assertions of independent personhood.

Accordingly, the six-layer model is not a finished safety standard. It is a framework for questions arising from ordinary AI collaboration. Future observation could record confirmation frequency, task completion time, operational errors, user fatigue, and the effectiveness of scoped permission memory.

## 14. Conclusion

The AI Kill Switch Act is not limited to one binary button: its sponsors describe throttling, suspension, and full shutdown. This paper therefore does not argue against the proposal. It extends the same graduated logic into everyday operations, where warning, confirmation, approval gates, speed and permission limits, temporary isolation, and emergency shutdown form a continuum.

The Mori Research Lab case suggests that AI already applies many brakes. The unresolved problem is calibration. Too little braking creates accidents; too much braking prevents useful work and forces the human to repeat the very decisions the AI was meant to reduce. The desirable state is one in which routine low-risk permissions are remembered appropriately, while consequential actions still trigger a meaningful pause.

> AI does not need only a button that is pressed once at the end.<br>It needs brakes that can be applied continuously—and the ability to choose how to stop.

At present, Mori Research Lab may already have more brake operators than accelerators. Before installing another emergency switch, it may be necessary to loosen some of the ordinary brakes and continue observing the result. 😂
