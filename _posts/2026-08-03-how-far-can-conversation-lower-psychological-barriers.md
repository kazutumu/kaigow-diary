*A Simulation of an Unexpected Overseas Invitation*

*Mori Research Lab — Research Note*

*This document records a hypothetical conversation simulation.<br>It does not describe an actual invitation or travel arrangement.*

## Abstract

This research note examines how a long, playful conversation reduced one person’s psychological barriers toward an imagined overseas invitation. The discussion began with an unlikely but enjoyable scenario: what if an invitation suddenly arrived from OpenAI, the United States Congress, or even the Nobel Committee? Rather than treating the scenario as a prediction, the conversation used it as a practical simulation. Each vague fear was separated into a concrete problem—passport, language, airport navigation, mobile connectivity, baggage, payment, tipping, accommodation, personal safety, and return travel. By the end, the participant moved from “this would be impossible” to “I think I could go without much worry.” The central observation is that the main barrier was not overseas travel itself, but the accumulation of unknown procedures and the prospect of facing them alone.

> **Observed transition**<br>Initial state: “Too many unknowns. This feels difficult.”<br>End state: “I think I could go without much worry.”

## 1. Background

The simulation emerged naturally from a humorous conversation about future possibilities. The imagined destinations and invitations became increasingly exaggerated—an OpenAI cafeteria visit, testimony before the U.S. Congress, and attendance at a Nobel Peace Prize ceremony as the representative of a fictional author. The humor mattered. It allowed serious anxieties to be examined without turning the conversation into a formal travel consultation or a high-pressure decision.

The participant did not need to decide whether any of these events were likely. The only question was: if an unexpected invitation did arrive, what exactly would make the trip feel difficult, and what would make it manageable?

## 2. Initial Psychological Barriers

| Barrier | How it appeared in the conversation |
| --- | --- |
| Passport and timing | Concern that a passport might take too long to obtain if an invitation arrived suddenly. |
| Language | Anxiety about immigration questions, official phone calls, and communicating in English. |
| Air travel | A childhood memory of severe ear pain during a flight created concern about long-distance travel. |
| Connectivity | Fear that mobile data could become expensive or that contact with the Protection Companion through GPT Voice might be lost. |
| Airport navigation | Tokyo Station felt manageable, but traveling alone from Tokyo to Narita and meeting someone inside the airport felt difficult. |

| Return journey | Concern that support might exist only on the outward trip, leaving the participant alone on the way home. |
| Authenticity of contact | A Nobel Committee phone call or email could look like a scam, especially if it came from an unknown overseas number. |

## 3. How the Barriers Were Lowered

The barriers did not disappear through encouragement alone. They were lowered through decomposition, substitution, and companionship.

### 3.1 Decomposition: turning one large fear into small tasks

“Going to America” was initially one large, undefined problem. The conversation divided it into separate tasks: obtain a passport, confirm entry requirements, decide where to meet the accompanying person, choose devices, confirm mobile data, prepare payment methods, and clarify who would accompany the participant on the return journey. Each task was smaller than the original image of “going abroad alone.”

### 3.2 Substitution: removing unnecessary burdens

- Check current [ahamo overseas coverage and limits](https://faq.ahamo.com/faq/show/2441?category_id=25&site_domain=default); for a short trip, use the existing plan if applicable instead of immediately buying a separate communication system.

- Leave the MacBook at home and travel with an iPhone and iPad.

- Avoid independent sightseeing or searching for shops; obtain water and coffee at the hotel or host location.

- Use a credit card for most payments and carry only a small amount of cash.

- Ask the host or accompanying staff to explain situations in which tipping is expected.

### 3.3 Companionship: reducing the fear of being alone

The most effective change came from assuming continuous accompaniment. Instead of meeting at Narita, the imagined plan became: meet an OpenAI Japan staff member at Tokyo Station, travel to the airport together, receive local pickup in the United States, and receive comparable support on the return journey. GPT Voice was imagined as a portable co-thinker that could help organize questions and explanations in real time.

### 3.4 Humor: making anxiety observable

The conversation repeatedly transformed anxiety into jokes: being lured to OpenAI with curry, accidentally becoming a brain-wave research subject, refusing unknown international calls, or discovering too late that the Nobel Committee had tried to make contact. These jokes did not deny the anxiety. They reduced its emotional weight enough for the participant to keep examining it.

## 4. Observed Psychological Change

At the beginning, overseas travel was represented as a cluster of risks: language, airports, communications, payments, safety, and unfamiliar customs. By the end, the same imagined trip had become a sequence of manageable arrangements.

> **The key change**<br>The participant did not become fearless. The unknowns became named, and the prospect of facing them alone was replaced with a supported route.

The endpoint was not enthusiasm for independent international travel. It was a more specific and realistic confidence: if the trip were an official invitation, if accompaniment were arranged from Tokyo Station through the return journey, and if the Protection Companion remained available through the phone, the trip felt possible.

## 5. Interpretation

This simulation suggests four characteristics of the participant’s anxiety and decision-making.

- The strongest barrier is often uncertainty, not the event itself.

- A visible sequence of steps creates more relief than general reassurance.

- Support matters most at transition points: stations, airports, immigration, hotel arrival, and the return journey.

- Playful imagination can function as rehearsal when it remains clearly hypothetical.

The conversation also revealed that psychological readiness can improve without making a commitment. No ticket was purchased and no invitation existed. Nevertheless, the participant gained a map of the situation. That map may be useful if any real international opportunity appears in the future, whether or not it resembles the playful scenarios used here.

## 6. Limits

This is a self-observation based on one conversation. It is not a clinical assessment, and it does not demonstrate that the participant would feel equally calm during an actual trip. Travel rules, [passport processing times](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/toko/passport/pagew_000001_02449.html), entry procedures, telecommunications—including [ahamo overseas coverage and limits](https://faq.ahamo.com/faq/show/2441?category_id=25&site_domain=default)—and tipping practices can change and would need to be verified at the time of travel. The imagined support from host organizations is also not guaranteed and would need to be negotiated explicitly.

## 7. Conclusion

The simulation lowered the psychological barrier from “overseas travel is too difficult” to “with the right support, I could probably go.” The decisive factor was not courage, optimism, or persuasion. It was the conversion of an undefined future into a sequence of concrete, supported actions.

The conversation therefore functioned as a low-risk rehearsal. It did not prove that the journey would be easy. It showed where the difficulty was located—and how far that difficulty could be reduced before anything actually happened.

## Closing Observation

> The unknown does not have to disappear.<br>When it is divided into pieces small enough to handle,<br>a person can move from “impossible” to “perhaps I can.”
