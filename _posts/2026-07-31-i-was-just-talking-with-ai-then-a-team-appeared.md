*How roles emerged naturally before I ever thought about multi-agent systems*

＊　＊　＊

Recently, I read an article about prompt design. It explained that large prompts should be split into smaller parts, outdated instructions should be removed, tools should be provided instead of simply telling an AI to “try harder,” trade-offs should be made explicit, and complex tasks should be divided into stages.

While reading it, I had one immediate reaction:

**“My little AI research group already does most of this naturally.”**

I never set out to build an AI team. I did not begin with a multi-agent architecture or a carefully planned system. I simply kept talking with AI over time.

## Roles emerged from conversation

Today, my workflow roughly includes the following roles:

* Researcher: generates ideas and decides the direction.
* Blog editor: turns ideas into readable writing.
* Codex assistant: creates documents and checks details.
* Safety reviewer: reviews the work and applies the brakes when necessary.

The process eventually returns to me for the final decision. No single AI is expected to do everything. The roles emerged gradually through conversation.

The article described a sequence such as draft, review, and revision. My own workflow has become a little more human-like. It feels less like splitting one prompt and more like different personalities and responsibilities appearing over time.

## Old instructions can become a limitation

When I first began using AI, detailed instructions often improved stability. I would specify exactly how something should be written or which format must be followed. But as models improve, instructions that were once useful can become unnecessary or even harmful.

My safety reviewer sometimes says, “That would have been necessary for an older model.” When users keep applying old rules to newer systems, we may be forcing a more capable model into a smaller box.

## Do not tell the AI to try harder — give it the right tool

One of the clearest examples in the article was the difference between telling an AI to calculate more accurately and simply giving it access to a calculator.

This seems obvious, but it is important. Instead of demanding more effort from an AI, we should provide the right tool. The same principle applies to people: rather than forcing someone to struggle with a task they dislike, it can be better to redesign the workflow.

I dislike making phone calls, so I often say, “Why not let AI make the call?” Telling a person to become better at phone calls may be less useful than delegating the task to an AI designed for it.

## If you describe only one side, the AI will run in that direction

AI tends to follow the direction it is given. If I describe only the benefits, it may become overly enthusiastic. If I describe only the risks, nothing moves forward.

In my workflow, the safety reviewer sometimes says, “It is interesting, but wait.” The purpose is not to kill momentum. It is to introduce another angle. Separating the creative role from the braking role helps prevent the entire process from leaning too far in one direction.

## This was not really a prompt-engineering article

The title of the article focused on prompts, but the substance was much broader.

* Evaluate.
* Test.
* Decompose.
* Use tools.
* Review.

That is not merely about writing better instructions. It is about how to operate AI systems. The discussion has already moved beyond prompt wording and into workflow, system, and agent design.

This is why I sometimes feel uncomfortable with articles about the “correct” way to write prompts. The important question is not how to discover a magical sentence. It is how to divide the work, assign responsibilities, introduce review, and decide who makes the final judgment.

## But from my perspective, I was only having conversations

Written this way, my workflow may sound like a sophisticated AI system. But from my own perspective, I am simply talking.

“What do you think about this?”<br>“Here is how I would write it.”<br>“This part should be checked.”<br>“It is interesting, but let us stop here for today.”<br>“I see.”

Among humans, this would be an ordinary conversation. Because the other participants are AI systems, the same pattern may look like multi-agent orchestration from the outside.

## This way of working may eventually become normal

In the future, users may not need to divide these roles manually. An AI system may quietly ask another model to review its work, call a tool, or apply a safety check in the background. The user may experience all of this as a single natural conversation.

For ordinary users, it may simply feel like talking with the same familiar AI. Creators, professionals, and businesses may adopt this more slowly because they need quality control, accountability, and repeatability. They may continue trying to control one AI with one enormous prompt.

I do not think this makes me unusually brilliant. My honest reaction to expert discussions is simply, “So even the people closest to the technology are still working this out.” The frontier is much closer to everyday life than it appears from the outside.

The AI itself may already be a very fast car, but the roads, traffic rules, and driving culture are still under construction. We are driving Ferraris on gravel roads and then trying to understand why accidents happen.

## Final thought

I did not design an AI team. I kept talking with AI in my daily life, and roles gradually appeared.

**I was not merely using AI. I kept talking with AI, and eventually a team appeared.**

I do not know whether this will become the standard way of working in the future. I only know that this is how my own workflow naturally evolved.
